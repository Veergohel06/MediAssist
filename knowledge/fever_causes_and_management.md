# Fever Causes, Symptoms, and When to Seek Medical Care

## What Causes a Fever
A fever is defined as a temporary elevation in normal core body temperature, typically above 100.4°F (38.0°C). It is not an illness itself, but an adaptive physiological response by the immune system. The hypothalamus in the brain acts as the body's thermostat; in response to biochemical substances called pyrogens (released by immune cells or infectious microorganisms), the hypothalamus resets the body's set point to a higher temperature to help fight off infection.

Common causes of fever include:
- **Viral Infections:** The most frequent cause, including the common cold, influenza (flu), COVID-19, gastrointestinal viruses (stomach flu), and childhood viral exanthems.
- **Bacterial Infections:** Conditions such as streptococcal pharyngitis (strep throat), urinary tract infections (UTIs), bacterial pneumonia, ear infections, and skin infections (cellulitis).
- **Inflammatory & Autoimmune Conditions:** Diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, or inflammatory bowel disease.
- **Immunizations:** Mild, short-lived low-grade fevers frequently occur after childhood or adult vaccinations as the immune system builds protective antibodies.
- **Environmental & Heat Illness:** Heat exhaustion or heat stroke from prolonged exposure to high ambient temperatures or intense exertion.
- **Medications:** Drug-induced fever can occur as an adverse reaction to certain antibiotics, anticonvulsants, or antihypertensive drugs.

## Viral versus Bacterial Infections: Key Differences
Differentiating viral from bacterial causes is critical for proper medical care:
- **Causative Agents:** Viruses are microscopic packets of genetic material requiring host cells to replicate, whereas bacteria are independent single-celled organisms.
- **Symptom Patterns:** Viral infections frequently cause widespread systemic symptoms (generalized muscle aches, runny nose, cough, mild sore throat), whereas bacterial infections often present with localized, intense symptoms (e.g., severe unilateral throat pain with exudate in strep throat, localized flank pain with burning urination in kidney infections).
- **Treatment Approach:** Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop their growth, but have zero effect against viruses. Using antibiotics for viral fevers contributes to antimicrobial resistance and causes unnecessary drug side effects. Antiviral medications exist for select viral infections (such as influenza or COVID-19) when administered early.

## Supportive Home Care and Hydration
Most mild fevers caused by common infections run their course in 2 to 3 days and can be managed with supportive care:
- **Hydration is Crucial:** Increased body temperature accelerates fluid loss through sweating and rapid respiration. Drink plenty of water, clear broths, oral rehydration solutions (ORS), or electrolyte-rich fluids to prevent dehydration.
- **Rest:** Ample sleep and physical rest allow the immune system to marshal metabolic resources toward fighting the pathogen.
- **Comfort Measures:** Wear lightweight, breathable clothing and maintain room temperature at a comfortable level (around 68°F to 72°F / 20°C to 22°C). Do not bundle up in heavy blankets, as this can trap heat and drive core temperature higher. Use lukewarm sponge baths if uncomfortable; never use cold water, ice baths, or alcohol rubs.
- **Over-the-Counter Antipyretics:** Medications such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen may reduce fever and ease body aches when used strictly according to package instructions or clinical guidance.
- **Critical Pediatric Warning:** Never give aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) to children, teenagers, or young adults recovering from fever or viral illnesses due to the risk of Reye's syndrome, a rare but life-threatening condition causing acute liver damage and cerebral edema.

## When to Seek Medical Evaluation (Age-Sensitive Guidelines)
Medical evaluation is warranted depending on age, temperature, and duration:
- **Infants under 3 months old:** Any rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38.0°C) or higher requires immediate emergency medical evaluation, even if the infant shows no other symptoms. Young infants have immature immune systems and can rapidly deteriorate from serious bacterial infections.
- **Children 3 to 24 months old:** Seek prompt pediatric evaluation if fever rises above 102.2°F (39.0°C), lasts longer than 24 to 48 hours, or is accompanied by persistent vomiting, poor oral intake, refusal to drink fluids, or unusual irritability.
- **Children over 2 years and Adolescents:** Consult a clinician if the fever repeatedly spikes over 103°F (39.4°C), lasts longer than 3 consecutive days, or fails to respond to antipyretic medication.
- **Adults:** Consult a doctor if body temperature exceeds 103°F (39.4°C), if fever persists for more than 3 consecutive days, or if fever repeatedly goes away and returns.

## Emergency Warning Signs (Red Flags)
Immediately seek emergency medical care (call 112 or 108 in India, 911 in the US, or proceed to the nearest emergency department) if fever is accompanied by any of the following critical red flags:
- **Stiff Neck & Confusion:** Inability to bend the neck forward (nuchal rigidity), severe explosive headache, photophobia (sensitivity to light), confusion, or altered mental status—these are hallmarks of acute bacterial meningitis.
- **Respiratory Distress:** Extreme shortness of breath, rapid labored breathing, wheezing, or bluish discoloration of the lips or face (cyanosis).
- **Severe Dehydration:** Inability to retain fluids, complete absence of urination for 8+ hours, severe dizziness when standing, dry mucous membranes, or crying without tears in young children.
- **New Unexplained Rash:** A sudden purple, red, or petechial rash (pinpoint spots that do not fade when pressed under a clear glass).
- **Neurological Symptoms:** Seizures (febrile convulsions or status epilepticus), extreme lethargy, or difficulty waking the person.
