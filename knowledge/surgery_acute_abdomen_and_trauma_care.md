# General Surgery: Evaluation of Acute Abdomen, Appendicitis, and ATLS Principles

## Clinical Approach to the Acute Abdomen and Peritoneal Signs

The term "acute abdomen" designates rapid-onset, non-traumatic abdominal pain typically requiring urgent evaluation and potential emergency surgical intervention.

### 1. Neuroanatomy of Abdominal Pain
- **Visceral Pain:** Mediated by unmyelinated C-fibers innervating the visceral peritoneum and muscular walls of hollow viscera. Triggered by stretch, ischemia, or inflammation. Pain is characteristically dull, crampy, poorly localized, and referred to midline embryological zones:
  * Foregut structures (stomach, duodenum, biliary tree, pancreas) $\rightarrow$ Epigastrium.
  * Midgut structures (small bowel, appendix, ascending and proximal two-thirds of transverse colon) $\rightarrow$ Periumbilical region.
  * Hindgut structures (distal third of transverse colon, descending colon, sigmoid, rectum) $\rightarrow$ Suprapubic hypogastrium.
- **Parietal (Somatic) Pain:** Mediated by myelinated A-$\delta$ sensory fibers innervating the parietal peritoneum. Stimulated by direct contact with bacterial exudates, gastric acid, or blood. Characterized by sharp, intense, constant, precisely localized pain.
- **Referred Pain:** Convergence of visceral afferent fibers with somatic sensory neurons in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord (e.g., diaphragmatic irritation from subphrenic fluid or ruptured spleen referred to the left shoulder tip via the phrenic nerve C3–C5, termed **Kehr sign**).

### 2. Hallmarks of Peritonitis
- **Voluntary Guarding:** Voluntary contraction of abdominal wall musculature due to fear, anxiety, or cold hands; abates when the patient is instructed to bend their knees or breathe through an open mouth.
- **Involuntary Rigidity:** Involuntary, reflex spasm of abdominal wall muscles ("board-like rigidity") indicative of established parietal peritonitis.
- **Rebound Tenderness (Blumberg Sign):** Sudden withdrawal of manual palpation pressure elicits severe localized pain due to stretch of inflamed parietal peritoneum.
- **Percussion Tenderness:** Gentle percussion of the abdomen produces localized sharp pain; often preferable to vigorous manual rebound testing.

## Acute Appendicitis: Pathogenesis, Signs, and Alvarado Score

Acute appendicitis is the most frequent acute surgical emergency worldwide, with a lifetime incidence of approximately 7–8%.

### 1. Etiopathogenesis and Symptom Sequence
- **Obstruction of the Appendiceal Lumen:** The initiating event in $> 60\%$ of cases is obstruction, caused by a **fecalith** (appendicolith) in adults, or **lymphoid follicular hyperplasia** (frequently following viral gastroenteritis or respiratory infections) in children and adolescents. Less common causes include foreign bodies, parasites (*Enterobius vermicularis*), or carcinoid tumors.
- **Intraluminal Pressure Rise & Ischemia:** Ongoing mucus secretion behind the obstruction raises intraluminal pressure, exceeding venular and capillary perfusion pressures $\rightarrow$ mucosal ischemia and bacterial invasion (*Escherichia coli*, *Bacteroides fragilis*).
- **Classic Migratory Pain Pattern (Murphy's Triad):**
  1. *Early Phase:* Luminal distension stimulates visceral afferent T10 nerves, causing dull, vague periumbilical pain accompanied by anorexia, nausea, or vomiting.
  2. *Intermediate Phase:* Inflammation progresses through the appendiceal wall to involve the adjacent parietal peritoneum (after 12–24 hours), shifting pain to become sharp, constant, and exquisitely localized in the **Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ)** at **McBurney's Point** (one-third the distance along a line drawn from the anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus).
  3. *Late Phase:* Gangrene and micro-perforation develop, potentially leading to a localized appendiceal phlegmon/abscess or generalized purulent peritonitis.

### 2. Physical Examination Signs
- **McBurney's Sign:** Maximum localized tenderness over McBurney's point.
- **Rovsing's Sign:** Palpation and continuous deep pressure in the left lower quadrant (LLQ) produces referred pain in the right lower quadrant due to displacement of intraluminal gas stretching the cecum and appendix.
- **Psoas Sign:** Extension of the right hip joint with the patient in the left lateral decubitus position stretches the iliopsoas muscle; localized RLQ pain suggests an inflamed **retrocecal appendix**.
- **Obturator Sign:** Passive internal rotation of the flexed right hip with the knee at 90 degrees stretches the internal obturator muscle; localized hypogastric pain suggests an inflamed **pelvic appendix**.
- **Dunphy's Sign:** Increased RLQ pain elicited by coughing.

### 3. Alvarado (MANTRELS) Scoring System

| Category | Clinical Feature | Score Value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **S**ymptoms | **M**igration of pain to RLQ | 1 |
| | **A**norexia (or acetone in urine) | 1 |
| | **N**ausea or vomiting | 1 |
| **S**igns | **T**enderness in right lower quadrant | **2** |
| | **R**ebound tenderness in RLQ | 1 |
| | **E**levated temperature ($\ge 37.3^\circ\text{C}$ / $99.1^\circ\text{F}$) | 1 |
| **L**aboratory | **L**eukocytosis (WBC $> 10,000/\mu\text{L}$) | **2** |
| | **S**hift to the left (neutrophils $> 75\%$) | 1 |
| **Total Score** | | **10** |

- *Score 1–4:* Appendicitis very unlikely; observe or seek alternate diagnosis.
- *Score 5–6:* Compatible with appendicitis; admit for serial physical examinations and ultrasound or CT imaging.
- *Score 7–8:* Probable appendicitis; proceed to surgical exploration / appendectomy without unnecessary diagnostic delay.
- *Score 9–10:* Almost definite appendicitis; immediate surgical intervention indicated.

## Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO) versus Paralytic Ileus

| Clinical Parameter | Mechanical Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO) | Functional Adynamic (Paralytic) Ileus |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Etiology** | Postoperative peritoneal adhesions ($> 60\%$), incarcerated hernias, neoplasms, intussusception, gallstone ileus | Postoperative physiological cessation of peristalsis (24–72 h), peritonitis, electrolyte disturbances (severe hypokalemia), opioids, retroperitoneal hematoma |
| **Pain Character** | Severe, colicky, paroxysmal abdominal pain coinciding with visible or audible peristaltic rushes | Dull, generalized, continuous discomfort or painless distension |
| **Auscultation** | High-pitched, tinkling, metallic bowel sounds or borborygmi during pain paroxysms | Absent or markedly hypoactive bowel sounds ("silent abdomen") |
| **Radiological Findings (Erect X-ray / CT)** | Multiple air-fluid levels in a "step-ladder" pattern, dilated small bowel loops ($> 3\text{ cm}$) with plicae circulares (valvulae conniventes) spanning the entire diameter, and collapsed colon distal to obstruction | Diffuse, uniform gas distension throughout both small and large bowel without a demonstrable mechanical transition point |
| **Strangulation Indicators** | Fever, tachycardia, localized continuous pain, leukocytosis, metabolic acidosis (elevated lactate), and bowel wall thickening with pneumatosis intestinalis on CT | Rare unless secondary to generalized intra-abdominal catastrophe |

## Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) Primary Survey: The ABCDE Framework

The primary survey identifies and treats immediate life-threatening injuries sequentially according to the principle of "treat first what kills first."

### 1. Airway Maintenance with Cervical Spine Protection
- Assess patency by speaking to the patient; a clear verbal response indicates an intact airway, adequate phonation, and brain perfusion.
- Clear foreign material, secretions, or blood using rigid suction.
- Maintain manual in-line stabilization (MILS) of the cervical spine in neutral alignment until radiological and clinical clearance. Avoid cervical hyperextension or rotation; use the **jaw-thrust maneuver** rather than the head-tilt/chin-lift.
- Establish a definitive airway (cuffed endotracheal tube in the trachea) if the airway is compromised, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) $\le 8$, or respiratory failure is imminent. If endotracheal intubation fails in a "cannot intubate, cannot ventilate" scenario, perform an emergency **surgical cricothyroidotomy**.

### 2. Breathing and Ventilation
- Expose the chest and evaluate respiratory rate, chest wall symmetry, work of breathing, and tracheal position.
- Immediately recognize and manage life-threatening thoracic conditions during the primary survey:
  * **Tension Pneumothorax:** Hypotension, distended neck veins, unilateral absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation to contralateral side. Immediate treatment: Needle thoracostomy with a 14G cannula at 5th intercostal space anterior to mid-axillary line (or 2nd intercostal space mid-clavicular line), followed immediately by formal tube thoracostomy (chest drain).
  * **Open Pneumothorax ("Sucking Chest Wound"):** Seal with a three-sided occlusive flutter-valve dressing.
  * **Flail Chest:** Paradoxical chest wall movement with underlying severe pulmonary contusion; provide high-flow oxygen, analgesia, and positive pressure ventilation if needed.
  * **Massive Hemothorax:** Dullness to percussion, shock, absent breath sounds; evacuate via large-bore chest tube ($28\text{ to }32\text{ Fr}$) and prepare for emergent thoracotomy if initial drainage $> 1500\text{ mL}$ or $> 200\text{ mL/hr}$ for 2–4 hours.

### 3. Circulation and Hemorrhage Control
- Identify and control external hemorrhage via direct manual pressure and proximal tourniquets. Internal bleeding sites evaluated by the **"Blood on the Floor and Four More"** rule: Chest, Abdomen/Pelvis, Retroperitoneum, and Long Bone fractures.
- Perform **eFAST (extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma)** to detect hemoperitoneum, hemothorax, and pericardial tamponade (Beck's triad: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds).
- **Classification of Hemorrhagic Shock:**
  * *Class I:* Blood loss $< 15\%$ ($< 750\text{ mL}$); normal vitals, minimal anxiety.
  * *Class II:* Blood loss $15\text{ to }30\%$ ($750\text{ to }1500\text{ mL}$); tachycardia ($HR > 100$), normal BP, decreased pulse pressure, tachypnea ($RR 20\text{–}30$).
  * *Class III:* Blood loss $30\text{ to }40\%$ ($1500\text{ to }2000\text{ mL}$); marked tachycardia ($HR > 120$), hypotension, oliguria, confusion.
  * *Class IV:* Blood loss $> 40\%$ ($> 2000\text{ mL}$); severe tachycardia ($HR > 140$), profound hypotension, anuria, lethargy/coma.
- Resuscitation: Initiate balanced **Damage Control Resuscitation (DCR)** with warm blood products in a 1:1:1 ratio (Packed Red Blood Cells, Fresh Frozen Plasma, Platelets) rather than excessive crystalloid infusions to prevent the lethal trauma triad (hypothermia, coagulopathy, metabolic acidosis). Administer **Tranexamic Acid (TXA)** within 3 hours of injury.

### 4. Disability (Neurological Evaluation)
- Rapidly evaluate pupil size, symmetry, and light reactivity.
- Calculate the **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** score across Eye Opening (E1–E4), Verbal Response (V1–V5), and Motor Response (M1–M6), ranging from 3 to 15.

### 5. Exposure and Environmental Control
- Completely undress the patient to perform a head-to-toe examination, including log-rolling to inspect the posterior thoracic and lumbar spine and rectal exam if indicated.
- Prevent hypothermia immediately using warm blankets, heated intravenous fluids, and controlled room temperature.
